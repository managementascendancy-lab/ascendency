## Where to actually start

If you're starting from zero, the order you learn things in matters more than how hard you push on any one of them. This roadmap is the sequence: get your setup right first, then your fingers, then your speed — in that order, not reversed.

## Step 1: Fix your setup before you fix your typing

Wrist position, desk height and elbow angle affect your ceiling more than most beginners expect, and bad habits formed here are harder to unlearn later than they are to avoid now. Keep wrists straight, not bent, and elbows close to your body at roughly 90 degrees. The full breakdown — including why a bent wrist is the single biggest contributor to long-term strain — is in the [keyboard ergonomics guide](/guides/keyboard-ergonomics); read it before you build up serious practice volume, not after.

## Step 2: Learn home row before you learn speed

Every finger has a home key, and every other key is reached by moving out from home row and immediately returning to it. Resist the urge to look down — every glance reinforces hunt-and-peck instead of the muscle memory you're trying to build.

## Step 3: Expect a slowdown before you see progress

If you're switching from hunt-and-peck to real touch typing, your WPM will very likely drop before it climbs. This is normal, not a sign you're doing something wrong — you're replacing a familiar but limited pattern with an unfamiliar but scalable one, and unfamiliar is always slower at first.

## Step 4: The plateau is where most beginners quit

Somewhere after the first few weeks, progress will flatten out even though you're still practicing. This plateau is usually a sign that finger placement has become automatic but speed hasn't caught up to it yet — the fix is deliberate, focused practice sessions rather than more casual volume. The [drills that actually move your WPM](/guides/improving-your-wpm) cover exactly this stage.

## Step 5: Practice cadence matters more than session length

Short, frequent sessions build the underlying motor pattern faster than occasional long ones. Five focused minutes a day beats one exhausted hour once a week, especially early on — fatigue degrades the exact precision you're trying to train.

## Put the roadmap into practice

Once your setup and home row feel solid, run a [simulation](/simulator) at 15 or 30 seconds and see where you currently classify. That number is your real starting point — everything from here is measured progress against it, not a guess.

Here's a worked version of Steps 3 and 4 above. Say you're currently hunt-and-peck typing at 30 WPM and switch to proper touch typing this week. It's realistic to dip into the low 20s WPM for the first week or two — you're relearning finger placement from scratch, and unfamiliar is always slower than familiar, even when familiar was worse. By around week 5 you'll typically climb back past your old baseline, and somewhere around weeks 6-8 progress usually flattens into a plateau even with regular practice — that's the stage Step 4 describes. What breaks the plateau isn't more volume at the same intensity, it's shifting to deliberate, focused sessions: short bursts specifically targeting whichever keys or patterns still make you hesitate, rather than just running more simulations at the same pace.

If you haven't locked in the finger map itself yet, back up to our [touch typing basics guide](/guides/touch-typing-basics) before pushing through the plateau — the curve above assumes the finger map is already automatic, not still being learned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for my WPM to actually go down when I start this roadmap?
Yes — that's Step 3, and it's one of the most common reasons beginners give up early. You're replacing a familiar hunt-and-peck pattern with an unfamiliar touch-typing one, and unfamiliar is always slower at first even though it has a much higher ceiling. Expect it, and don't take it as a sign you're doing something wrong.

### How do I know if I'm actually on the plateau or just not practicing enough?
If your finger placement already feels automatic — you're not looking down and not consciously thinking about where each key is — but your WPM still isn't climbing, that's the plateau from Step 4. If you're still hesitating over finger placement, you're not on the plateau yet; you're still working through Step 2.

### Should I practice every day or just a few times a week?
Step 5 covers this directly: short, frequent sessions build the underlying motor pattern faster than long, infrequent ones. Five focused minutes daily outperforms one long session once a week, especially at this early stage.

### How do I actually know if the roadmap is working, beyond just watching WPM go up?
Run a [simulation](/simulator) periodically and check your result against the [hero classification system](/guides/understanding-hero-classification). Because classification tracks WPM, accuracy and consistency together, it shows you which of the three the roadmap has actually moved — useful, since a rising WPM with falling accuracy isn't the progress it looks like on the surface.

### What if I keep hesitating on the same few keys no matter how much I practice?
That's usually a sign the practice itself isn't targeted enough — general typing volume reinforces what you already do well more than it fixes a specific weak spot. Deliberately typing words and short phrases that lean on exactly those keys, slowly and correctly, tends to close the gap faster than more general-purpose practice at your usual pace.
