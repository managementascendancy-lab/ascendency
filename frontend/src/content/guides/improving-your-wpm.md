## Speed is a side effect, not a goal

The biggest mistake people make when trying to type faster is chasing speed directly. WPM is a byproduct of two things: how confidently your fingers know where the keys are, and how little your brain has to consciously translate a word into a sequence of keystrokes. Chase those two things and the number takes care of itself.

## 1. Slow down before you speed up

If you're making more than 2-3 errors per 100 characters, you're already typing too fast for your current skill level. Every error costs you far more time than typing slightly slower would have — you have to notice it, stop, backspace, and re-type. Run a few simulations at a pace where your accuracy sits above 97%, then gradually push the pace up.

## 2. Fix your weakest fingers, not your whole hand

Almost everyone has one or two fingers doing more than their fair share of work — usually the index fingers — while the ring and pinky fingers lag behind. Pay attention to which characters make you hesitate. If it's always `q`, `p`, `z`, or punctuation, that's a pinky problem, and it's worth deliberately practicing words that lean on those keys rather than avoiding them.

## 3. Type in chunks, not letters

Slow typists process one letter at a time. Fast typists recognize common letter clusters — "tion", "ing", "the", "and" — as single motor patterns. This comes from repetition, not effort. The more volume you type, the more these patterns get burned in automatically.

## 4. Consistency beats bursts

A run where you hit 90 WPM for three seconds and 40 WPM for the rest isn't actually a 90 WPM run — and Ascendancy's [consistency metric](/guides/understanding-hero-classification) will call that out. Smooth, sustained pace is both faster on average and a better sign of real skill than spiky bursts followed by recovery.

## 5. Warm up before a serious attempt

Just like any physical skill, your hands benefit from a short warm-up. Run one or two easy simulations before attempting a personal best — cold fingers make more mistakes, and mistakes are the real WPM killer.

## Put it into practice

Head to the [Training Simulator](/simulator) and run a 60-second session focused purely on accuracy first. Once you're consistently above 97%, gradually push your pace on the next few attempts. Progress compounds — small, steady gains beat one-off lucky bursts.

Once you've set a new personal best, it's worth having proof of it — your [Profile](/profile) page lets you download an official WPM certificate, based on your best recorded run, anytime you want it.

## Worked example: why "faster but sloppier" usually loses

Picture two 60-second runs. Run A types at a raw 50 WPM pace but averages 6 errors per 100 characters — well above the 2-3 error guideline from tip #1. Run B types at a raw 45 WPM pace with roughly 1 error per 100 characters. Each error costs roughly 3-4 keystrokes of overhead to notice, backspace and re-type. At 6 errors per 100 characters, Run A is burning something like 18-24 extra keystrokes per 100 just on corrections — time that never shows up as "typing" but still eats into the run. Run B's near-clean pace carries almost none of that overhead. The raw 5 WPM gap Run A appears to have going in gets eaten by correction time, and its final accuracy score takes the hit besides. That's the same math tip #1 is built on, just walked through with numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many errors per 100 characters is actually "too many"?
As a rough guide, staying above 97% accuracy — roughly under 3 errors per 100 characters — is a reasonable target while you're still building speed. Above that, correction overhead usually costs more time than the extra speed saves.

### Which finger should I focus on if I don't already know which one is weak?
Pay attention to which characters make you hesitate rather than guessing. If it's consistently punctuation, `q`, `p`, or `z`, that's almost always a pinky issue — see tip #2 above.

### Is chunking something I can practice directly, or does it just happen naturally?
It comes from repetition more than deliberate effort — the more volume you type, the more common clusters like "tion" and "ing" get burned in as single motor patterns automatically. High-volume, focused practice sessions build this faster than consciously trying to "chunk" mid-run.

### How is consistency different from just having a high average speed?
They're not the same thing at all — consistency measures how steady your pace is relative to your own average, not whether that average is high. See the [full breakdown of the consistency score](/guides/understanding-typing-consistency-score) for exactly what it tracks and why bursty runs score lower even at the same average WPM.

### Should I focus on speed or accuracy first if I can only work on one?
Accuracy, without much competition — it's the foundation the other two metrics are built on. The [full case for prioritizing accuracy](/guides/typing-accuracy-guide) walks through exactly why a fast, sloppy run consistently underperforms a slower, clean one.
