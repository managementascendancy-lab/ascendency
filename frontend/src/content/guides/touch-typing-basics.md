## What touch typing actually means

Touch typing is typing without looking at the keyboard, using a consistent mapping of fingers to keys so your hands can move on muscle memory alone. It's the single biggest unlock for anyone starting from hunt-and-peck typing — most of the WPM ceiling in early-stage typing isn't about finger speed, it's about the time lost glancing down and re-finding your place.

## Learn the home row first

Rest your left hand on `A S D F` and your right hand on `J K L ;`. Your index fingers stay on `F` and `J` — most keyboards have a small raised nub on both keys so you can find them by feel. Every other key is reached by moving a finger out from home row and immediately returning to it.

## The standard finger map

- **Left pinky**: `Q A Z` and the far-left keys
- **Left ring**: `W S X`
- **Left middle**: `E D C`
- **Left index**: `R F V` and `T G B`
- **Right index**: `Y H N` and `U J M`
- **Right middle**: `I K ,`
- **Right ring**: `O L .`
- **Right pinky**: `P ; /` and the far-right keys
- **Thumbs**: spacebar

It feels unnatural at first, especially for the pinky fingers. That's normal — it improves quickly with repetition and gets worse if you revert to whichever finger is "easiest" in the moment.

## Resist the urge to look down

Every time you glance at the keyboard to confirm a key, you reinforce hunt-and-peck habits and lose the time savings touch typing is supposed to give you. If you genuinely don't know where a key is yet, it's faster long-term to pause, think about the finger map, and get it right blind than to peek and get it right by sight.

## Expect a temporary slowdown

If you're switching from an established hunt-and-peck habit to proper touch typing, your WPM will likely drop before it improves. This is expected — you're replacing a familiar-but-limited pattern with an unfamiliar-but-scalable one. Push through the first week or two; the ceiling on the other side is much higher.

## Where to practice

Start with short, low-pressure [simulations](/simulator) at 15 or 30 seconds, focused entirely on correct finger placement rather than speed. Once the finger map feels automatic rather than effortful, gradually introduce longer sessions and let speed follow naturally.
